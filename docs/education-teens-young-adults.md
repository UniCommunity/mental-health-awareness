# Education for teenagers and young adults

Mental health is how you think, feel, and handle stress, relationships, school, work, and change. Everyone has mental health, the same way everyone has physical health. It can be strong, strained, or in need of care.

This page is **psychoeducation**, not a diagnosis. Only a qualified clinician can diagnose a condition.

## What "normal stress" vs. "I need help" can look like

Life as a teen or young adult includes exams, breakups, identity questions, money stress, family conflict, and social comparison. Struggle is common. Get extra support when problems:

- Last **weeks to months**, not just a bad day
- Interfere with sleep, eating, school, work, or friendships
- Include hopelessness, feeling like a burden, or thoughts of death
- Include panic attacks, self-injury urges, hearing/seeing things others don't, or using substances to cope every day

You can ask for help *before* things become a crisis.

## Common conditions (plain language)

These descriptions follow public NIMH / CDC / WHO educational materials.

### Anxiety
Persistent worry, restlessness, muscle tension, racing thoughts, avoidance of situations, panic (sudden intense fear with physical symptoms). Anxiety disorders are among the most common conditions in adolescence.

### Depression
Low mood or irritability most of the day, loss of interest in things you used to care about, sleep or appetite change, fatigue, guilt, trouble concentrating, feeling worthless. Depression in teens can look more like irritability or withdrawal than "sadness."

### ADHD
Ongoing patterns of inattention and/or hyperactivity-impulsivity that began in childhood and affect school, work, or relationships. Not the same as "being distracted by phones."

### Trauma-related stress
After a frightening, violent, or violating event, some people develop intrusive memories, nightmares, jumpiness, numbness, or avoidance. Trauma-focused therapies have evidence; you do not have to "just get over it."

### Eating disorders and body image distress
Not only about food. Involves control, shame, and health risk. Early professional help matters.

### Psychosis (less common, important)
A break from shared reality: hearing voices, strong false beliefs, confused thinking. Early treatment improves outcomes. This is a medical emergency if new and severe.

### Substance use
Alcohol, cannabis, nicotine/vapes, and other drugs interact with mood and anxiety. Using to "shut off" feelings is a signal to talk to someone, not a moral failure.

Co-occurrence is common. Treatment plans can address more than one thing.

## Protective factors you can strengthen

- At least one **trusted adult**
- **Sleep** (most teens need ~8-10 hours; young adults often need 7-9)
- Regular **movement** and daylight
- Friendships that are not only online
- Skills for naming emotions and solving problems
- Limits on late-night unstructured social media
- Purpose: clubs, faith or values communities, art, sport, volunteering, meaningful work

These do not cure illness by themselves. They make recovery more likely and crises less likely.

## Social media and the developing brain

The U.S. Surgeon General and APA have advised that social media is not neutral for adolescents. Risks rise with night-time use that steals sleep, appearance comparison, cyberbullying, crisis content, and displacement of in-person connection.

Practical habits many clinicians suggest: no phones in bed; mute accounts that leave you feeling worse; time-box apps; talk with a trusted adult if content is harming you.

## How treatment usually works

1. A school counselor, university counseling center, GP / pediatrician, or community clinic
2. Screening questions (not a pop quiz you can fail)
3. Talk therapy (CBT and interpersonal therapy have strong evidence for adolescent depression and anxiety)
4. Sometimes medication, decided with a prescriber after discussing benefits and side effects
5. Skills groups, peer support, family sessions

Asking for an appointment is a complete first step.
