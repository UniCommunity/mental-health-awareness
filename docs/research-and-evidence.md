# Research and evidence

This is a map of **what the literature generally supports**, written for non-specialists. It is not a systematic review. Prefer primary guidelines when making policy.

## Mental health in adolescence and young adulthood

- Onset is early. A large share of lifetime mental disorders begin in adolescence (WHO; NIMH summaries).
- Treatment gaps are large worldwide (WHO / PAHO).
- Return on investment for treating and preventing adolescent mental disorders is high in modeling studies (one widely cited analysis estimated roughly $24 in benefits per $1 invested over a long horizon, with variation by country and intervention).

## Treatments with strong evidence for youth depression and anxiety

- **Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT)** — well-established for adolescent depression in individual and group formats; also first-line for many anxiety presentations.
- **Interpersonal therapy (IPT)** — well-established for adolescent depression.
- **Trauma-focused CBT** — supported for trauma-exposed youth with PTSD symptoms.
- **Medication** — for moderate-to-severe depression, guidelines often discuss SSRIs in combination with therapy, with monitoring (decisions belong to a prescriber and family).
- **COPE** and similar manualized CBT skills programs have been delivered at scale in primary care and schools.

Universal classroom programs tend to improve **literacy** more reliably than they reduce clinical depression scores. Skills-based, multi-session programs outperform one-off talks. Indicated programs (for students already showing symptoms) usually show larger effects.

## Suicide prevention evidence (high level)

Relatively **stronger** evidence:

- Restricting access to highly lethal means — repeatedly the most robust population finding.
- Follow-up contact after a suicide attempt.
- Safety planning plus structured care, versus informal "promise me you won't."
- Some school peer-leader models (Sources of Strength has RCTs showing increased help-seeking norms).

**Promising / mixed:** gatekeeper training, media guidelines, crisis lines as part of systems of care.

**Not supported:** no-suicide contracts as a standalone safety strategy; graphic fear-based assemblies as "prevention"; the myth that asking about suicide plants the idea.

See WHO LIVE LIFE (2021) and CDC Suicide Prevention Resource for Action (2022).

## School mental health literacy

Systematic reviews find that school programs can improve knowledge and sometimes reduce stigma in the short term. Combining education with contact (lived experience, live or filmed) often works better for attitudes. Booster sessions help; one workshop fades.

## Community and integrated youth models

- **headspace** (Australia) and similar one-stop shops for ages ~12-25 increase access; outcome research is mixed depending on severity and wait times.
- Near-peer navigator models (e.g., Youth Mental Health Corps) are early-stage but aligned with connection-to-care goals.
- Programs that emphasize peer connection + structured activity + adult support align with protective-factor research.

## Social media

Evidence is associational. Risks concentrate in sleep disruption, social comparison, harassment, and displacement of offline support (Surgeon General Advisory; APA health advisory).

Apps can be useful bridges. They are not a replacement for care when someone is severely depressed or suicidal.
