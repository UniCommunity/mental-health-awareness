# Advice commonly shared by healthcare professionals

This is general psychoeducation distilled from public clinical guidance and standard therapeutic skills (CBT, IPT, behavioral activation, sleep medicine). **It is not personalized medical advice.** If symptoms are severe, start with a clinician or crisis line.

## 1. Treat sleep as a mental health intervention

Irregular, short, or inverted sleep worsens anxiety, depression, and suicide risk.

- Same wake time daily, including weekends as much as you can stand
- Light in the morning; dim screens late
- Bed is for sleep, not scrolling
- If you cannot sleep after ~20-30 minutes, get up and do something quiet until sleepy
- Caffeine cutoff mid-afternoon

If nightmares or insomnia persist, ask about CBT-I.

## 2. Move your body, even a little

Exercise is not a replacement for therapy in moderate-severe depression, but it reliably helps mood and anxiety for many people. A 10-minute walk counts.

## 3. Behavioral activation (for depression)

Depression tells you to isolate and wait until you *feel* like living. Schedule small valued actions first; mood often follows later. Track what you did, not how inspired you felt.

## 4. Name the thought pattern

- Thoughts are not facts
- "Should" statements and mind-reading are common in anxiety
- Catastrophizing can be tested against evidence
- Problem-solving: define the problem, list options, pick one small next step

## 5. Feelings are information, not orders

Anxiety says avoid. Gradual exposure with support is a first-line idea for many anxiety disorders. Do not flood yourself alone with your worst fear as a challenge.

## 6. Substances make the dip deeper

Alcohol is a depressant. Cannabis can increase anxiety or psychotic-like symptoms in vulnerable people. Using nightly to fall asleep usually backfires. Be honest with a clinician.

## 7. Connection is treatment-adjacent

One reliable person is more useful than 400 followers. Facilitated peer support helps when it is not a competition over who is suffering more.

## 8. Ask for the kind of help you need

You can say: "I think I might be depressed. Can I get screened?" or "I'm not safe to be alone tonight." Clinicians would rather have a clumsy ask than silence.

## 9. Safety planning beats willpower

A written plan typically includes warning signs, internal coping, people to contact, professionals to contact, making the environment safer, and reasons for living.

## 10. Recovery is uneven

Relapse of symptoms is common and does not erase progress. Return to the last plan that worked.

## When professionals urge urgent care

Go to emergency services or a crisis line if there is a plan and intent to die, psychosis with inability to care for self, medical instability, or violence risk.
